The second story: Ethan, the maximiser

Another made-up person, with a worked example. The rules and rates are the 2026 ones. The result is a projection, not a promise. Last checked: 9 October 2026.

Ethan starts his first job at 22 on $5,000 a month. In this example he never gets a pay rise, so he earns the same as Mdm Lim, Mr Tan and Mr Ong in the first story. His friend "Default Dan" earns the same and does only what is automatic. Ethan uses every CPF option open to him. We follow both men to 65.

The short version

  • At 65, Ethan's Retirement Account holds $652,492 and Dan's holds $326,246. Ethan's is exactly double.
  • The reason is one move at 55. Ethan puts $440,800 into his Retirement Account and Dan puts in $220,400. Both amounts come from money already in their CPF.
  • Ethan also adds $64,000 of his own cash in his first eight working years. In this example he did not really need it: Dan could make the same move at 55 with $0 of cash.

Where do these Special Account balances come from?

Not from Ethan's $64,000 of cash alone. Cash is only a small part. The balances at 55 are made up like this:

Default DanEthan
Own cash top-ups$0$64,000
Moved in from his own Ordinary Account (his CPF money, not new cash)$0$97,719
CPF's monthly share into his Special Account over 33 years$143,958$143,958

For Ethan, $64,000 + $97,719 + $143,958 + $479,469 = $785,146. About 61% of it is interest, because his money sits in the Special Account earning 4% for up to 33 years. If the $64,000 were the only money, it would grow to only about $204,369 by 55.

Ethan has $652,492 − $326,246 = $326,246 more. It is exactly double because the Enhanced Retirement Sum is exactly double the Full Retirement Sum.

What happens on their 55th birthday

Dan

  • His Special Account ($275,086) closes. The first $220,400 goes into his new Retirement Account.
  • The rest, $275,086 − $220,400 = $54,686, goes into his Ordinary Account. It now holds $651,729 + $54,686 = $706,415.
  • He does nothing more. At 65 his Retirement Account is $220,400 × 1.480244 = $326,246.

Ethan

  • His Special Account ($785,146) closes. The first $220,400 goes into his new Retirement Account.
  • The rest, $785,146 − $220,400 = $564,746, goes into his Ordinary Account. It now holds $451,512 + $564,746 = $1,016,258.
  • He moves another $220,400 from his Ordinary Account to his Retirement Account. It is now $220,400 + $220,400 = $440,800, the Enhanced Retirement Sum. His Ordinary Account keeps $1,016,258 − $220,400 = $795,858.
  • At 65 his Retirement Account is $440,800 × 1.480244 = $652,492, the same as Mr Ong's.
Show how Ethan's balances at 30 and 55 are worked out
  • Ages 22 to 29. Every January Ethan adds $8,000 of cash to his Special Account and moves his Ordinary Account savings in as well. That is 8 top-ups, so his own cash is 8 × $8,000 = $64,000.
  • At 30 his Special Account holds $225,098, a little above the $220,400 limit on top-ups. Interest and CPF's own monthly contributions can still take it slightly above the limit. His cash top-ups end here.
  • Ages 30 to 55. His SA grows by itself. $225,098 at 4% for 25 years is $225,098 × 2.665836 = about $600,074. CPF also adds $115,200 to his SA over those 25 years, which with interest becomes about $185,072. Together that is $785,146.
  • His Ordinary Account starts to fill again after 30: $13,960 plus $301,800 of monthly contributions, plus 2.5% interest, gives $451,512 at 55.
  • Dan's balances come from the same monthly contributions for 33 years: $412,200 into his Ordinary Account and $144,000 into his Special Account, plus 2.5% and 4% interest.

So what does Ethan's effort really buy?

Look at Dan's Ordinary Account: it holds $706,415 after his Special Account closes. That is far more than the extra $220,400 he would need to match Ethan. If Dan moves $220,400 from his Ordinary Account to his Retirement Account at 55, his Retirement Account becomes $440,800 and grows to $652,492 at 65. He needs no cash to do it, and his Ordinary Account still holds $486,015.

So the gap between the two men comes from one move at 55, not from Ethan's $64,000 of cash. Ethan does have more CPF money in total at 55: $1,236,658 (Retirement Account plus Ordinary Account) against Dan's $926,815. The reasons are that money in the Special Account earns 4% instead of 2.5%, and the $64,000 of cash.

This example assumes neither man uses CPF money for a home. Most people do, which leaves less in the Ordinary Account to move. Check your own balances before relying on this move.

Why Ethan's monthly payout at 65 is the same as Mr Ong's, about $3,440

The Enhanced Retirement Sum is the most CPF lets you set aside. Both men have it in their Retirement Accounts from 55, so their balances at 65 are the same ($652,492) and so are their estimated payouts. The $3,440 is CPF Board's own estimate for the Enhanced Retirement Sum (for a male member on the CPF LIFE Standard Plan).

Ethan's bonus option: wait until 70

CPF lets you start your CPF LIFE payouts anywhere from 65 to 70. Each year you wait, your monthly payout can rise by up to 7%, and your Retirement Account keeps earning 4% while you wait. If you do nothing, payouts start by themselves at 70. Say Ethan lives on other income until 70 and does not touch his Retirement Account.

Start payouts at 65Start payouts at 70
Retirement Account balance$652,492$793,856 ($652,492 × 1.216653)
Estimated monthly payout for lifeabout $3,440about $4,600
Difference$4,600 − $3,440 = about $1,160 more every month, which is $1,160 × 12 = about $13,900 more a year, for life
  • Where $4,600 comes from: CPF says that for a 55-year-old man with the Full Retirement Sum ($220,400) on the Standard Plan, starting at 70 gives about $600 more a month than starting at 65. Next to the $1,780 payout at 65, $600 is about 34%. We apply the same 34% to Ethan's $3,440, so the $4,600 is our estimate, not CPF's.
  • Five more years of interest: $793,856 − $652,492 = $141,364, without Ethan adding anything.
  • The catch: Ethan gives up five years of payouts at the lower amount: $3,440 × 12 months × 5 years = $206,400. He gains about $13,900 a year, so he gets that back after $206,400 ÷ $13,900 = about 15 years, meaning at about 85. He only comes out ahead if he lives past that. CPF itself says deferring pays off only if you live long enough, and that he needs another income from 65 to 70.

The working, if you want to check it

Show how the monthly CPF split works

CPF takes 37% of wages up to age 55 (17% from the employer and 20% from the employee). On $5,000 a month that is 37% × $5,000 = $1,850 a month. This is the official 2026 split:

AgeTo OA each monthTo SA each monthTo MediSave (not used here)
35 and below$1,150$300$400
Above 35 to 45$1,050$350$450
Above 45 to 50$950$400$500
Above 50 to 55$750$575$525

For example, at 35 and below the OA gets 62.17% of $1,850, which is $1,150 a month, or $13,800 a year ($1,150 × 12). The SA gets $300 a month, or $3,600 a year.

Show how "money put in" and "interest" are split

CPF does not label individual dollars. To split a balance, we assume that when money moves from one account to another, it carries the same mix of money-put-in and interest that the account had. On that basis, of the $440,800 in Ethan's Retirement Account at 55, about $201,500 is money put in and about $239,300 is interest already earned.

What this example leaves out

  • Interest: 4% a year on the Special and Retirement Accounts and 2.5% on the Ordinary Account, credited once a year. These are the lowest rates CPF currently guarantees, so real results could be higher. The extra interest on the first $60,000 of combined balances is left out.
  • Rules used: before 55, cash top-ups and moves from the Ordinary Account to the Special Account can go up to the Full Retirement Sum. At 55 the Special Account closes, up to the Full Retirement Sum goes to the Retirement Account, and the rest goes to the Ordinary Account. From 55 you can move Ordinary Account savings into the Retirement Account up to the Enhanced Retirement Sum.
  • Retirement sums: held at the 2026 amounts. They rise every year, so the same method would give larger figures in future dollars with less buying power.
  • Home: neither man uses CPF money for a home, which most people do.
  • Left out: MediSave balances, tax savings and the Government's extra interest.
  • This is a worked example from public rules, not a forecast and not advice. The figures come from our own model of those rules. Check your own numbers with the CPF Board's calculators.
